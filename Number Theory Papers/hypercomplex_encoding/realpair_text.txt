 Real-Pair Representations of Complex Numbers and Quaternions:
          Algebra, Arithmetic Coordinates, and Implementations in R and Python

                                                Fred Viole

                                           November 4, 2026


                                                 Abstract
          We study the real-pair transform ϕ(a + bi) = (a − b, a + b) and its blockwise extension to
     quaternions. These maps are scaled orthogonal changes of coordinates; complex multiplication
     and quaternion multiplication are transported to explicitly defined products on R2 and R4 ,
     rather than ordinary componentwise multiplication. We give exact identities for conjugation,
     multiplication, inversion, norms and exponentials. We also identify two specific arithmetic
     interpretations: ϕ(Z[i]) is an index-two parity sublattice, and the inverse map turns a factor pair
     (c, q) into a Fermat midpoint/half-gap complex coordinate whose square has real part cq. We
     describe the corresponding real-coordinate form of zeta-zero reflection without asserting any
     new zero-free theorem. Reproducible implementations in base R and standard-library Python
     provide complex and quaternion operations, worked examples, and deterministic and randomized
     verification. Together, the results give an exact coordinate dictionary for arithmetic, algebraic,
     and spectral computations.


1    Introduction and scope
Representing complex numbers by real coordinate pairs is a standard algebraic technique. This
paper develops the particular transform (a, b) 7→ (a − b, a + b) as a common coordinate system for
complex and quaternion arithmetic, integer factor-pair geometry, and spectral calculations. Explicit
transported operations make the representation usable in real-valued computational environments,
including R and Python.
    The selected coordinates are especially natural for factor-pair bookkeeping: they recover the
two factors in Fermat’s difference-of-squares parameterization directly from midpoint and half-gap
coordinates. The same representation preserves normalized Gram products and expresses complex
spectral modes through real vectors without altering their zeros or analytic properties. Sections 2–4
prove the identities; Sections 3 and 5 explain the arithmetic and spectral interpretations. Code and
verification procedures and the companion source archive are described in Section 6.

5    Real coordinates for complex spectral parameters
The same transform can encode a complex spectral parameter without changing its analytic meaning.
If ρ = β + iγ and (u, v) = ϕ(ρ), then

                      u = β − γ,           v = β + γ,        ℜρ = 12 ⇐⇒ u + v = 1.                 (19)

The reflection ρ 7→ 1 − ρ becomes

                                           (u, v) 7−→ (1 − v, 1 − u).                              (20)

These are exact coordinate statements; they do not place any zeros on the critical line.
   For an illustrative homogeneous factor m = q 2ρ−1 with q > 0, Equation (7) gives the real-pair
squared-energy multiplier
                                         |m|2 = q 4β−2 .                                     (21)
At β = 1/2 this multiplier is one; at β > 1/2 it is greater than one for q > 1. Equation (21) concerns
an individual complex multiplier; turning it into a bound on an actual prime- or Möbius-weighted
sum requires additional arithmetic estimates.
    Lean formalizations of the real-pair interface, its Gram identity, and the factor-pair correspon-
dence are available in the RH_Lean repository, notably realification module and Fermat-coordinate
module. That project also includes a conditional square-endpoint reformulation of the von Koch
prime-counting criterion. The present note does not prove the Riemann hypothesis or an improved
prime-counting error bound; those are separate questions.


6    Implementation and verification
The supplied files Real_Pair_Representations.R and Real_Pair_Representations.py imple-
ment complex real-pair and quaternion real-quadruple arithmetic using base R and the Python
standard library, respectively. Conversions to and from each language’s native complex type are
restricted to phi and phi_inv; the remaining arithmetic functions use real-valued vectors or tuples.

                                                         4

This facilitates use with numerical code expecting real vectors, although no speed advantage over
native complex operations is claimed.
    Two numerical details matter. First, norm-squared expressions are scaled by the largest absolute
entry before inversion, reducing avoidable intermediate overflow/underflow in squared terms (without
guaranteeing accuracy at every floating-point extreme). Second, the quaternion exponential evaluates
sin r/r through its Taylor series near zero. We use exact zero checks for algebraic inverses rather
than silently rejecting valid small nonzero quaternions by an arbitrary absolute threshold.

    The R suite uses stopifnot, and the Python suite uses unittest. The test sources include
deterministic quaternion basis cases, both-sided inverses, Gaussian norms, factor-pair identities,
complex exponentials, and seeded randomized comparisons to an independent quaternion coefficient
formula. The Python tests require no third-party packages. All implementations and verification
scripts are freely available in the Number-Theory companion code directory.

7    Conclusion
The transform ϕ(a + bi) = (a − b, a + b) is a classical linear change of coordinates equipped with
explicitly transported operations. Its concrete value here lies in preserving complex and quaternion
algebra using real vectors, representing Gaussian integers as a parity lattice, and recovering Fermat
factor pairs directly from the midpoint/half-gap complex coordinate. Base-R and standard-library
Python implementations make the operations computationally reproducible, with explicit treatment
of quaternion inversion and the exponential near zero. Both companion test suites exercise the
transported algebra through deterministic examples and randomized identities. These coordinate
relations can support further arithmetic and spectral analyses, while a factorization algorithm or a
new zero-free theorem would require additional mathematical arguments.
